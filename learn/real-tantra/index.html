Learn · Tantra

Real tantra isn't what you think.

Say "tantra" and most people think of sex. But the classical tantric traditions of India were mostly about something else: breath, mantra, meditation, devotion, and the idea that every part of life, the body included, is sacred.

What classical tantra was

Tantra grew in India about 1,500 years ago. A "tantra" is a scripture, and the traditions built around them taught ritual, yoga, mantra, visualization and a philosophy that sees the whole world as an expression of the divine. Nothing is outside it, including desire.

Scholar-practitioner Christopher Wallis, author of Tantra Illuminated, says the popular idea of tantra as mainly about sex is simply untrue: almost none of the classical scriptures teach sexual techniques. (The Conversation, 2024)

Where "neo-tantra" came from

Western "neo-tantra" took shape around the start of the 20th century and focused on sacred sexuality. Historian Hugh Urban has traced how the word got tangled up with Western ideas about sex and money along the way. Classical scholars question how authentic it is. Neo-tantra teachers answer that they're living out tantra's core idea in the body. (The Conversation)

The thread that connects them

Both sides agree on one thing: the body isn't an obstacle to awakening. It's the doorway. Tantra's yoga practices are also where much of hatha yoga, and later modern yoga, came from. The breath, the locks, the energy rising up the spine: that's tantric technology.

What this means for you

  • Sexual energy is life energy. Tantra treats it as fuel to grow and move, not to suppress or spend carelessly.
  • The real practices are simple. Breath, sound, attention, the root locks and a reverent attitude toward your own body.
  • Watch out for gurus. A good test from Wallis: ask a teacher which tantric scripture they follow. If they can't say, be careful.

The glow hood connection
