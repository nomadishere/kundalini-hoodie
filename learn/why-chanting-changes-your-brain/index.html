Why chanting changes your brain.

Every tradition that works with energy also works with sound: Om in India, "Hu" in Sufism, chant in monasteries, toning in the Taoist healing sounds. It turns out the humming itself does measurable things.

Humming opens your sinuses

In 2002, researchers at the Karolinska Institute in Sweden measured nitric oxide, a gas that relaxes blood vessels and helps fight germs, in people's noses. Humming raised it about 15 times higher than a quiet exhale, because the vibration ventilates the sinuses where it's made. (Am J Respir Crit Care Med, 2002)

Om quiets the emotional brain

A 2011 brain-imaging study in India had volunteers chant Om inside an MRI scanner. During Om, parts of the brain's emotional system went quieter, including the amygdala and the hippocampus. Making a plain "ssss" sound did nothing similar. The researchers think the vibration felt around the ears may stimulate the vagus nerve, the body's main "calm down" line. It was a small pilot study, so treat it as a promising start. (International Journal of Yoga, 2011)

The long exhale is the secret

Chanting and singing stretch your exhale. Slow exhales are one of the most reliable ways to shift the nervous system toward rest. That may be part of why a room full of people chanting feels so different from a room full of people talking.

A sound for each center

Many traditions give each chakra its own seed sound, rising in pitch from root to crown: LAM, VAM, RAM, YAM, HAM, OM, and silence at the crown. The glow hood plays a tone for each center as its light comes on, so sound, light and attention move together.

Try it: three minutes of humming

  1. Breathe in slowly through your nose.
  2. Hum on a long, easy exhale with your lips closed. Feel the buzz in your face and skull.
  3. Finish with one long "Ommm," letting the "mmm" ring.
  4. Repeat for three minutes, then sit in the quiet.
