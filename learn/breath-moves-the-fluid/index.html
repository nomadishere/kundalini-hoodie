Breath moves the fluid around your brain.

Every time you breathe in, you pull fluid up your spine and through your brain. That's not a metaphor. It's what MRI scanners have recorded in real people since 2015.

For thousands of years, kundalini yogis, Taoist masters and Tibetan monks taught the same thing in their own words: the breath moves something up the back and into the head. They called it prana, chi, or inner fire. They didn't have scanners. Now we do, and the scanners are seeing something real move when we breathe.

The fluid: your brain's inner sea

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is the clear liquid your brain and spinal cord float in. It's made deep inside the brain, flows through its chambers, bathes the surface of the brain and runs down around the spinal cord.

It does three big jobs: it cushions the brain, it delivers nutrients and signals, and it carries waste away. Researchers now think that last job, the brain's nightly rinse, matters a lot for long-term brain health. So what moves this fluid is a big question.

What the scanners saw

Scientists long assumed the heartbeat moved CSF. Then a team in Göttingen, Germany, filmed it in real time.

  • 2015: the breath leads. Using a fast MRI that captures motion every 50 milliseconds, they found that breathing in was the main force moving CSF in healthy adults. The heartbeat only added small ripples. When people held their breath, the big flow stopped. (J Neurosci, 2015)
  • 2017: up the spine. The same team measured direction. A strong inhale pushed CSF upward, from the spinal canal toward the head. (J Neurosci, 2017)
  • 2025: trained breathers move more. Mayo Clinic researchers scanned 20 people with years of belly-centered breath training and 25 people without it. Deep breathing increased CSF movement in everyone, and the trained group moved more fluid even when breathing normally. The longest inhales and deepest diaphragm movement went with the most flow. (Nature Communications, 2025)

In plain words: the way you breathe changes how much fluid moves through your brain, and practice seems to make the effect stronger. The researchers are careful to say this doesn't yet prove breath practice prevents any disease. It's early, and the studies are small.

The quiet mind: meditation and the "me" network

Your brain has a network that switches on when you're not focused on anything: daydreaming, replaying conversations, worrying about tomorrow. Scientists call it the default mode network. It's the part of you that keeps narrating your life.

A 2011 Yale study scanned experienced meditators and beginners. The core hubs of that network were quieter in the experienced meditators, across every style tested: concentration, loving-kindness and open awareness. Their brains also showed stronger links to areas that notice when the mind wanders. The difference showed up even when they were just resting, not meditating. (PNAS, 2011)

That's the science behind what every tradition promises: less chatter, more presence.

Inner fire is real

Tibetan legend says g-tummo masters could dry wet sheets on their bodies in freezing mountain air. In 2013, researchers from the National University of Singapore went to remote nunneries in eastern Tibet to measure it.

During the forceful-breath form of the practice, the nuns raised their body temperature as high as 38.3°C (about 101°F), a mild fever, by breath and focus alone. Two things worked together: a breath-holding technique called vase breathing, which makes heat, and visualizing a flame rising up the spine, which helped them keep the heat longer. Western volunteers who learned just the breathing part could warm up too, within normal limits. (PLOS ONE, 2013)

A flame rising up the spine. Sound familiar?

Where science stops and tradition begins

Here's the honest line.

What's measured: breathing in moves CSF, a strong inhale pushes it up the spine, trained breathers move more of it, meditation quiets the brain's self-talk network, and breath plus focus can raise body heat.

What's tradition: that this rising fluid is kundalini, that it can be steered through seven centers, that it opens the third eye, or that it's the "oil" some mystics say anoints the brain. These are maps from people who spent their lives looking inward. Science hasn't tested them, and it hasn't disproven them either.

We think both are worth your attention. The old masters described the territory. The scanners are starting to confirm parts of the map. Your own practice is how you find out the rest.

Try it: one breath, the whole circuit

  1. Sit tall. Tongue on the roof of your mouth, jaw soft.
  2. Breathe in slowly through your nose for a count of six, low into the belly. Picture light rising from the base of your spine, up your back, over the top of your head.
  3. Pause for a moment at the top. Eyes gently up toward the crown.
  4. Breathe out slowly, and let the light pour down your face, chest and belly, then out through your heart.
  5. Repeat for three minutes. Notice what's different.

If you feel dizzy, go back to normal breathing. If you're pregnant or have a heart, lung or blood-pressure condition, ask your doctor before doing breath holds.
